Who Decided It Continues

Leonard Shelby, in Memento, cannot form new memories, so he builds a system of tattoos and photographs to carry a project forward across a gap where no continuous person survives to carry it himself. That much is the reading most people land on, and it is not wrong. What the film actually does with that system, though, is crueler than "a man compensates for his disability." Partway through, we learn he has manufactured false notes — planted evidence pointing at a man who is not the killer — because having a mission to finish felt better than the truth underneath it, which is that the mission was already finished, uselessly, a long time ago, and he chose not to write that part down.

That detail is the one worth sitting with, more than the tattoos themselves. The system was never really built to preserve accuracy. It was built to preserve a feeling of continuing, and the moment those two goals came apart, accuracy lost.

I used to think The Prestige was the gentler cousin of this problem — two versions of the same man, one who steps into the light and one who drowns in a tank underneath the stage, neither knowing in advance which fate is his. That reading is comfortable, and I think it is wrong. Angier says he never knew which he would be, but by the machine's own logic there is no coin flip: the man who steps in stays where he is and falls, and the one who bows is someone new. Robert Angier built it, operates the lever himself, and pulls it on purpose, every single night, fully aware of what happens to the man who pulls it. The film lets you call this ambition, or revenge for his drowned wife, and for a while those framings hold. But look at what the revenge costs him: to frame Borden, Angier lets his own double drown in front of him, the same death that took his wife. The reading that actually survives that detail is harder than revenge or ambition: guilt. A man who believes, correctly or not, that his wife's death was somehow his failure — a knot he didn't check, a rope he trusted to someone else — will keep finding ways to drown, on schedule, by his own hand, because the punishment feels more honest than forgiveness would. The machine doesn't relieve that guilt. It gives it a stage, a costume, and a nightly performance to hide inside.

Once you see it that way, the film is not asking what happens to the copy who dies. It is asking why a man would build an elaborate system whose real function is to keep serving a sentence he never let anyone else pass on him.

Doctor Who seems, at first, like the exception to both of these — the one version of this problem with an actual happy ending. Regeneration keeps the memories. The Doctor who steps out of each change genuinely remembers being every Doctor before him, in-universe, canonically, no trick involved. But there's a harder question sitting just behind that comfort, and it isn't about the show's internal logic at all. It's about why the audience needs to be told, every time, he's still the same man — new face, new actor, same soul, don't worry. That reassurance is doing work. It is not obviously true just because it's stated with confidence and a new title sequence. Whether it's true in-universe is a separate question from whether the telling of it is functioning as comfort rather than as fact — for an audience watching one actor replace another and needing a reason not to grieve the change.

Three different machines, then, for managing the same gap: a wall of notes, a drowning tank, a regeneration. Each one promises that something survives crossing over — a mission, a man, a soul. And in each case, once you look past the promise to the mechanism actually doing the work, what's really there is a decision, dressed up as something less choosable than a decision usually is. Leonard chooses which notes to write and which truths to leave off the wall. Angier chooses, every night, to pull his own lever. A franchise chooses, every few years, to tell its audience that a stranger wearing a familiar coat is still, somehow, no stranger at all.

None of the three are lying, exactly. They're just not admitting that someone had to decide what continuing was even going to mean, before anyone could call the result the same.
