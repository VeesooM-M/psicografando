title: Two Realities, One Generation
excerpt: Gen Z shows a wider political gender gap than any generation before it, in Argentina and Brazil as well as the wealthy democracies. Why it started is contested. What keeps it going may be a different question.
---

Generation Z has produced a gender gap in politics wider than in any previous generation. In the United States, Catalist's analysis of the 2024 election found Harris winning 63 percent of women aged 18 to 29 but only 46 percent of men the same age — a 17-point gap, the largest recorded for any age group across four presidential elections. Britain, Germany, Canada, and South Korea show the same shape, independently, with young women moving further left and young men holding steady or drifting right. So does Latin America: young male voters are widely credited with helping carry Javier Milei to power in Argentina, and Brazil's Gen Z polling shows a comparable split.

Where the picture thins out matters too. A Financial Times analysis, as secondary sources describe it, reportedly found the same divide in Tunisia and China. I found no comparable polling for Sub-Saharan Africa or South and Southeast Asia, so whether the pattern holds there is simply unknown to me — and one secondhand summary of Economist reporting suggests the gap is largest in the richest, historically most egalitarian economies, which would make the missing data more than a coverage problem. Documented across several continents is as far as I can honestly go; "global" is not.

What makes this genuinely strange isn't the direction. Every generation has had *some* difference between how men and women vote. What's strange is that this generation shares, almost by definition, the same formative years — the same recession anxieties, the same housing crisis, the same pandemic, the same internet. Two groups exposed to nearly identical external conditions are arriving at close to opposite political conclusions about what those conditions mean and who's responsible for them.

The researchers studying this don't agree on why, and I think the disagreement is worth taking seriously rather than picking a side to repeat. One strand, following women, points to sharply politicizing events — the Dobbs decision, Me Too — landing during Gen Z women's most formative political years, producing a markedly more progressive cohort than the women before them. Another, following men, describes a sense of being structurally overtaken: women have pulled ahead in educational attainment across most developed economies, and some researchers link the resulting anxiety to receptiveness toward online communities built around grievance and a rejection of feminism. A third, narrower piece of work — an Oxford study using the European Social Survey — tried to quantify one factor: measurable sexist attitudes among Gen Z men account for roughly 10 to 13 percent of the voting gap, after controlling for education, religion, and other demographics. That's a partial, specific estimate of one contributor, not an explanation of the whole gap, and using it as one would be asking a careful study to settle an argument it was never built to settle.

Separate from why the gap opened is a different question: what keeps it going. Here the evidence is thinner than the story people tell, so I'll mark which parts are documented. Pew's platform-use data, as relayed by writer Meredith Conroy, show women leaning toward Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok and men toward Reddit and X, so on an ordinary day the two groups are immersed in different ecosystems with different norms. And feeds can move fast: an Axios experiment reported that a new account was carried, within an hour, from mainstream political content to "red pill" material about gender roles. That is one test of one pathway, and I found no equivalent test for the other side. Some reporting even notes that young women are increasingly engaging with conservative content too, so the sorting is not tidy.

What the evidence supports is narrower than "the algorithm did it." One analysis from Canada's Pearson Centre calls algorithms a major explanation of the gap, yet describes their role as amplifying a divergence that already existed — and that is the distinction I'd keep: amplifier, not origin. People also steer their own feeds, so a difference in what each group sees may be as much a result of the split as a cause of it. But an engagement-optimizing system doesn't need a theory of what men and women should believe. It only needs to notice that each group responds to different material, and it will quietly build two different worlds out of one population without anyone intending it. None of that tells us which of the three origin stories is right, and I don't think it should be used as a tiebreaker. What it does suggest, if the amplification story holds, is something new: a cohort raised under nominally identical conditions may be splitting into two groups who are increasingly not shown the same reality to disagree about — and a gap like that could keep deepening even after whatever opened it has faded.
