title: How 43 Votes Became Thousands
excerpt: On the morning after Election Day, a U.S. Senate race in Mississippi was separated by 43 votes. By November 6 the lead was about 2,500. Nobody changed their vote. The count kept arriving.
---

In the early hours of November 4, the morning after Election Day, Democrat Scott Colom was ahead of Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith by 43 votes in Mississippi's U.S. Senate race, out of nearly a million cast, with almost all ballots counted. Hyde-Smith had led for most of the night. By the afternoon of November 6, the Associated Press had Colom ahead by about 2,500 votes, roughly 0.27 percent of all ballots cast. Nobody changed their vote. The ballots simply kept arriving, and a margin that looked like a coin flip on Wednesday looked like a lead on Friday.

A count in progress is not a random sample of the electorate. Ballots are tallied in the order counties finish, and counties differ: some are large and urban, some small and rural, and mail, absentee and affidavit ballots move at different speeds. Whatever order they arrive in is the order in which the margin is measured. In Mississippi, Colom closed the gap as late returns came in from Hinds County, which includes Jackson, and from counties in the Delta and the southwest that had not reported earlier in the evening. His lead then grew with nearly every batch of results.

It is the same fact that makes election-night models possible. In Brazil's runoff last month, an analyst projected the winner from the first 2.4 percent of ballots by comparing each polling place with its own history, which strips out the effect of arrival order. A raw partial count mixes two things: what voters chose and the order in which their ballots were counted. Only the first is the electorate's.

What matters now is not the margin but the number of ballots still in doubt. A lead means something only against that number. As of 4:45 p.m. Eastern on November 6, with an estimated 97 percent of the vote in, the AP counted close to 30,000 votes still to be tallied. Erasing a lead of 2,500 from a pool that size would take about 54 percent of it, assuming every one of those ballots is counted and valid. Mail ballots can arrive until November 10, and affidavit ballots can be cured until the same date. Hyde-Smith's lawyers have asked counties to separate and preserve all affidavit ballots, a step that signals a possible fight over them, and both campaigns have teams ready to observe the count. According to one analysis, about half of the counties with outstanding ballots have favored each candidate, so the remaining ballots could widen the lead, shrink it or leave it roughly where it is.

If the lead holds near 0.27 percent, history is on the leader's side. The nonpartisan group FairVote counted 37 complete statewide recounts in 7,437 general elections between 2000 and 2025, and only three changed the winner, all with original margins under 0.06 percent of the votes cast for the top two candidates. Colom's lead is more than four times that. An earlier FairVote analysis of the recounts from 2000 to 2019 put the average shift at about 430 votes. But Mississippi has no automatic recount and does not let candidates request one. A recount happens only if a court orders it, so any challenge would run through the courts, and the state has until December 3 to announce final results.

So the number that headlines carry, the margin, is a poor guide to what is settled. On November 4, 43 votes looked like a tie. By November 6, when the AP had not yet called the race, about 2,500 looked like a lead. In both cases the real question was how many ballots were still unaccounted for and how they would split. A count is a measurement still being taken, and a margin is only as good as the share of the measurement that is finished.
