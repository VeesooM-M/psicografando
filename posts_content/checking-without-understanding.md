title: Checking Without Understanding
excerpt: When an AI disproved a famous geometry conjecture in 2026, proof-checking software certified the result without understanding any of it. What the software couldn't certify is that the sentence it checked says what mathematicians think it says.
---

In 1946, the mathematician Paul Erdős asked a question that sounds too simple to be hard: if you scatter n points on a flat plane, how many pairs of them can sit exactly one unit apart? A simple grid gives about two such pairs per point, and cleverer arrangements do only slightly better. Erdős conjectured that nothing could do much better: the number of pairs could never outgrow the number of points by any fixed power, however small. For eighty years, nobody proved it and nobody broke it. On May 20, 2026, OpenAI announced that an internal model had found arrangements that break it. Growth like n to the power 1.0001 would already be enough; the model's first margin was far smaller than that, but any fixed margin is fatal to the conjecture.

Three separate jobs run through this story, and I'll keep them apart. Finding an answer. Writing that answer out in a language a computer can check, which mathematicians call formalizing it. And having the computer check it. The AI took part in the first two. The third belongs to a program that has no cleverness at all, only strictness.

I'm relying here on one mathematician's account, written in the middle of events: Kevin Buzzard, who works at Imperial College London, on his blog. The announcement came with statements from mathematicians who had been given early access and said they believed the argument. Buzzard's first reaction was a question. Nine years ago, he writes, he realized he no longer trusted many human mathematicians on technical details, and began arguing that mathematics should lean on proof-checking software. So he asked whether this result had been checked that way. It hadn't.

That software wasn't built for mathematicians. Lean was created in 2013 by Leonardo de Moura at Microsoft Research to prove that computer programs do exactly what they should, the way a geometry student proves a theorem. It works like a strict examiner: it accepts a proof only if every step follows from the previous ones by fixed rules. It cannot be charmed, rushed, or bored. Mathematicians adopted it later. In late 2020, the Fields Medalist Peter Scholze, unsure about a crucial step in one of his own proofs, challenged the Lean community to check it. They finished in less than a year.

The bottleneck was formalizing. A proof written for human readers skips steps that a reader fills in without noticing, and Lean accepts none of that, so every step must be spelled out. By one estimate, a blackboard proof can grow twenty to a hundred times longer that way. Mathlib, the community's shared library of formalized mathematics, took nine years to reach about 2.3 million lines. Checking a hard proof meant a team of human experts translating it by hand, for months or years.

This is where the AI changed things. Its first job was the answer itself. OpenAI's own account says the key ideas were well known to specialists in a distant field, algebraic number theory, and that it came as a great surprise that they bore on geometry in the plane. Its second job was the formalizing, the translation that used to cost human experts months or years. Within a week, a company reported a first version, but it took the argument's central number theory theorem on trust, so it only showed that if the theorem holds, the counterexample works. The complete version came on June 26, when an OpenAI researcher, Boris Alexeev, reported that he had steered the company's model through the whole thing: about 1.2 million lines in three weeks, roughly half the size of mathlib. The checker, Lean, is still the same strict examiner; what changed is who writes what it reads.

Notice what that pairing does. The AI doesn't have to be trusted to get every step right: Lean checks the proof against the statement it was given. Buzzard found the AI's code "sometimes horrible, although sometimes decent," and the checker still did its job, because it looks only at whether the steps are valid. Finding an answer and checking one are very different jobs, like a Sudoku: solving can take an hour, while checking a finished grid takes a minute, even for someone who couldn't solve it.

But the Sudoku comes with a catch. Suppose someone copies the puzzle's starting numbers wrongly: a perfect solution to the wrong puzzle proves nothing about yours. In Lean, the starting numbers are the statement, the formal sentence the proof is supposed to prove. When another AI-written counterexample arrived, on an old algebra question, as a 12-page argument, Buzzard refused to read it and asked for a Lean version. Hours later he had one. He scanned it for anything destructive, then spent under five minutes on three checks: that the statement is written only in standard terms that mathematicians already share, so it means what it appears to mean; that it really says a counterexample exists; and that the proof compiles. The first two are checks of meaning, which he made himself. The third is a check of validity, which the machine made.

So trust didn't simply move from people to machines. It was divided. The AI proposes the answer and writes it out. The checker certifies that the written proof is valid. People establish that the sentence being checked says what was meant. Understanding travels separately: within days of the announcement, Princeton's Will Sawin raised the model's first, astronomically small margin to about n to the power 1.014, a step that took understanding the construction, not just certifying it. Buzzard himself writes that the real value of this work is to give humans a better understanding of mathematics. Checking has been separated from understanding the proof. It hasn't been separated from understanding what was asked.
